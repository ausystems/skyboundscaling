Why is your website not getting leads?

Traffic without inquiries means leaks. Here are the seven we find most often in real audits, ordered by how much revenue each one returns when fixed.

Start with the right diagnosis

A website that gets traffic but no leads has a conversion problem. A website that gets no traffic has a visibility problem. They feel identical from the inbox, but the fixes are completely different, so check your analytics first: if fewer than a few hundred people visit each month, skip to the traffic section. If people visit and vanish, you have leaks. Here are the seven we find most often, in the order to fix them.

Leak 1: The five second failure

A visitor gives your homepage about five seconds to answer three questions: what do you do, who is it for, and why should they care. Most business homepages answer none of them above the fold, opening instead with a slogan the founder likes. Rewrite the first screen to name the service, the customer, and the outcome, in plain words a stranger would use. This one change moves more conversion numbers than any redesign flourish.

Leak 2: No single next step

Pages with five competing buttons convert like pages with none. Every page needs one primary action: book a call, request a quote, see pricing. Make it visually unmissable, repeat it as the page scrolls, and write it as an outcome. A button that says Book a call outperforms one that says Submit every day of the week.

Leak 3: Forms that interrogate

Every field you add to a form removes real people from your pipeline. Ask for what you need to respond well and nothing more: name, email, and a message beats eleven required fields and a dropdown about how they heard of you. Our own brief form asks only what a senior needs to reply usefully within a day.

Leak 4: Zero proof

Claims without evidence are decoration. Named clients, real numbers, recognizable logos, and case studies with outcomes are what let a stranger take the risk of contacting you. If your work is good and your site does not show it, your site is keeping secrets from the people it exists to convince. This is exactly why we publish case studies with names and numbers attached.

Leak 5: Mobile decay

More than half your visitors are on phones, where sloppy sites collapse: text too small, buttons too close, forms that fight autocorrect, three second loads that feel like ten. Open your site on your own phone, on cellular data, and try to contact yourself. If anything annoys you, it is turning away buyers daily. Speed matters double here: pages that load in under two seconds hold visitors that slower pages lose before the first word renders.

Leak 6: The trust gaps

Small signals quietly kill conversions: no address or city, no faces, stock photography pretending to be your team, a copyright year from two years ago, a broken link in the footer. None of these is fatal alone. Together they whisper that nobody is home. Sweep them quarterly.

Leak 7: Nobody follows up fast

The form works, the lead arrives, and the reply goes out two days later, after the buyer hired someone else. Speed to lead is a conversion lever most businesses ignore: instant confirmation, fast human follow up, and automation that routes inquiries to a person who owns them. We wire this into every funnel we build, because the fastest responder wins an outsized share of deals.

And if the problem is traffic

If nobody visits, conversion polish cannot save you. The two reliable engines are search and paid: SEO builds compounding visibility for the terms your buyers search, and Google Ads buys immediate volume while SEO matures. Our comparison of Google Ads vs SEO walks through which to start first for your situation.

Questions, answered

How many leads should a website generate?

It depends on traffic and industry, but a useful floor for a service business is a conversion rate of two to five percent of visitors taking a contact action. Below one percent almost always means fixable leaks from this list.

What is the fastest fix on this list?

The first screen rewrite. Naming what you do, who it is for, and the outcome, above the fold, routinely moves conversion within days because every single visitor sees it.

Do I need a redesign to fix conversion?

Often no. Message, proof, forms, and speed can frequently be fixed inside the current design. When structure itself is the problem, our redesign checklist covers doing it safely.

How do you measure which leak is costing the most?

Analytics and session behaviour: where visitors land, how far they scroll, where they exit, and which pages precede conversions. The week one audit in our process maps exactly this.
